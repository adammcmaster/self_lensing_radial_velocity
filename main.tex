% Abstract of the paper
\begin{abstract}
    Compact objects in approximately edge-on binary orbits with stars are known to be able to produce a self-lensing effect, amplifying light from their companion once per orbit. To date, five white dwarf self-lensing systems are known, while none are known containing black holes or neutron stars. Discovery of such systems is a potential method of finding quiescent black holes and neutron stars, most of which will be pristine and unchanged from their formation. Photometric detections alone can suffer from degeneracies in the lens mass and orbital parameters when the peak amplification and crossing time are not well constrained, while photometric searches produce too many false positives to allow detailed radial velocity investigation of every candidate. Therefore we investigate the minimal radial velocity (RV) observational requirements to characterise such systems by simulating detectable self-lensing light curves, using those light curves to generate targeted RV observations, and fitting RV jointly with photometry. We evaluate the median relative error in the recovered lens mass and orbital period, alongside compact-object class recovery, and map the fitted outcomes onto population-synthesis predictions for current and future photometric surveys. We find that adding RV measurements substantially reduces parameter error, with compact-object class generally easier to determine than the exact lens mass. Parameter accuracy is driven mainly by number of RV observations, with RV precision playing a weaker role except for the longest-period systems. These results suggest that multi-stage RV follow-up, starting with a small number of observations of many candidates, can efficiently classify self-lensing candidates and identify high-priority systems for further investigation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
There are expected to exist vast numbers of compact objects in binary systems in the Milky Way \citep[e.g.][]{shao_population_2019,wiktorowicz_noninteracting_2020,weller_mapping_2023}. The majority of these are not close enough to interact with their companions, making them challenging to detect. This is especially true of black holes, which would produce negligible electromagnetic emissions in such systems.

The detection of gravitational microlensing is a proven technique \citep{paczynski_gravitational_1986,paczynski_gravitational_1996,mao_astrophysical_2012}, in which the gravity of a foreground object (the \textit{lens}, which is often not visible) amplifies flux from a background object (the \textit{source}). This has been used to detect and study various classes of object, including exoplanets \citep{bond_ogle_2004,gaudi_microlensing_2012} and black holes \citep{sahu_isolated_2022,lam_isolated_2022}. Self-lensing is a special case of microlensing where the source and the lens are in a binary system together, in an orbit which is close to edge-on, producing a periodic microlensing amplification of the source once per orbit \citep{maeder_light_1973,agol_occultation_2002}. Self-lensing is only possible when the lens is a compact object. This means a detection of self-lensing (i.e. periodic microlensing) is by definition a detection of a compact object.

A self-lensing search is an intriguing way to search for compact objects in binaries which would be undetectable by other means. To date, five self-lensing binaries have been confirmed, each containing a white dwarf lens, all of which have relatively close, short-period orbits \citep{kruse_koi-3278_2014,kawahara_discovery_2018,masuda_self-lensing_2019}. However, it is expected that stellar-compact object binaries should span the full range of compact object masses, as well as the full range of orbital configurations.

Photometry remains the best way to detect self-lensing binaries, and previous works have assessed the detectability of self-lensing binaries in various photometric surveys \citep{wiktorowicz_predicting_2021}. These results look promising, with modern all-sky surveys expected to imminently uncover a population of compact objects in self-lensing binaries, including the currently under-studied population of quiescent black holes.

While detection of self-lensing systems with photometry alone has been demonstrated, modelling of such systems and characterisation of the compact component can be subject to several degeneracies between the physical parameters which can make it challenging to accurately determine the mass of the lens in cases where the amplification due to self-lensing, the crossing time, or the stellar companion properties are not well known (see Figure~\ref{fig:self-lensing-observable-space} for these relationships). For short-period binaries it should be possible to derive a photometric radial velocity (RV) by modelling Doppler boosting \citep{loeb_periodic_2003,zucker_beaming_2007,van_kerkwijk_observations_2010,faigler_photometric_2011}, but for systems with no detectable Doppler boosting effect, spectroscopic observations are the best way to solve for the binary's physical parameters \citep{kawahara_discovery_2018,yahalomi_mass_2019}. This is especially true for systems with the longest orbital periods, for which we may only be able to observe a single lensing event in a human lifetime (i.e. those with periods lasting decades, centuries, or more).

In this work, we consider the question of how many spectroscopic RV measurements are required, and to what level of precision, in order to accurately retrieve the self-lensing system parameters as a function of those parameters. This includes systems with orbital periods on the order of centuries for which the orbital period can not be determined photometrically. This will inform observing strategies for follow-up of the large number of candidates expected to come from new surveys, allowing the most efficient use of resources. We also consider the usefulness of photometric-only fitting to guide RV observing strategy.

We will show that it is possible to accurately model very long period self-lensing binaries with a handful of RV measurements, even when periodicity can not be observed. In many cases, photometric-only fits can come close to determining the properties, removing the need for many RV measurements to retrieve the parameters accurately.

\section{Methods}

We investigate the relationship between the number and precision of RV measurements and the relative error in recovered physical parameters as a function of lens mass, compact object type, and orbital period. We evaluate parameter accuracy using the median relative error, and separately consider recovery of broad compact-object class (i.e. distinguishing black holes from other compact objects even if the fitted mass remains imprecise).

As the basis of this work, we have generated a library of self-lensing photometric simulations covering a range of physical parameters, filtered to include only the simulated light curves which contain visible lensing, where the peak amplitude is larger than the photometric noise. We also exclude systems overflowing their Roche lobe and any systems where lensing is not geometrically possible. These simulations are described in Section~\ref{sec:phot-sims}.

An MCMC fit is then performed against the simulated photometry, to sample the posterior distribution of the self-lensing physical parameters and find distinct physical solutions for each light curve. We assume the stellar properties, mass, radius, effective temperature, and distance from Earth, are known. The set of distinct solutions is then used to compute an RV observing schedule using a variable number of observations (between three and ten observations) over a fixed five-year campaign, such that each schedule is optimised to distinguish between the MCMC solutions.

Radial velocity measurements are then simulated for the computed observing schedule, using the same set of simulation parameters as the photometry (Section~\ref{sec:rv-sims}). Gaussian noise is added to simulate a range of spectrograph accuracies.

A second MCMC fit is then performed to sample the posterior distribution constrained jointly by the photometry and RV (Section~\ref{sec:fitting}. We then aggregate the median relative errors across observing parameters and self-lensing physical parameters to investigate how each observing schedule affects parameter retrieval.

Finally, we map the fitted outcomes onto previous population synthesis results to estimate the numbers of self-lensing systems which can be confirmed following detection in various photometric surveys (Section~\ref{sec:pop-synth}).

\subsection{Self-lensing and photometric binary effects} \label{sec:phot-sims}
We note that the photometric model described here is similar to the recently published COBALT-BLUE model (Dixon et al. in preparation) (\textbf{TODO: Update this citation before submission if possible}), but uses a simpler approximation to the full photometric forward model. Compared with COBALT-BLUE, our model adopts a local approximation to the eccentric self-lensing geometry and lower-order treatments of Doppler boosting and ellipsoidal variability, while adding the spectroscopic radial-velocity component to improve the recovery of lens mass and other physical parameters. We anticipate combining the two models in a future work.

Simulations comprise a sample of self-lensing system parameters which is physically complete but not physically realistic (i.e. parameters are not sampled according to the frequency they are expected to occur in nature). This allows us to determine the ranges of parameters which produce self-lensing effects, independent of how common or uncommon particular parameter ranges are expected to be.

The photometric simulations comprise realistic simulated TESS-like light curves (including stellar noise generated with a Gaussian process, see Section~\ref{sec:gp}) for each self-lensing system. When generating the simulations, the parameter values were sampled uniformly between the limits given in Table~\ref{tab:phot_sim_parameter_ranges}, with the exception of semi-major axis ($a$), eccentricity ($e$), and lens mass ($M_\mathrm{lens}$), which are sampled on a log scale to increase the number of samples at smaller values (the light curve morphology changes roughly in proportion to the relative change in these values; e.g. a 5 AU change in semi-major axis has a much larger effect going from 1 AU to 6 AU than for 100 AU to 105 AU).

\begin{table}
    \centering
    \caption{Parameter ranges used to generate the photometric simulation library.}
    \label{tab:phot_sim_parameter_ranges}
    \begin{tabular}{lll}
        \hline
        Parameter         & Description           & Range                      \\
        \hline
        $a$               & Semi-major axis       & 0.1--200 AU                \\
        $i$               & Orbital inclination   & $0.5\pi-10^{-4}$--$0.5\pi$ rad \\
        $M_\mathrm{lens}$ & Lens mass             & 0.5--50 M$_\odot$          \\
        $M_\star$         & Stellar mass          & 0.5--50 M$_\odot$          \\

        $d$               & Distance from Earth   & 0.1--100 kpc               \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Three photometric effects are included when generating the light curves: self-lensing, ellipsoidal variations, and Doppler boosting. At small values of $a$ the light curve is dominated by ellipsoidal variations and Doppler boosting (though lensing is often visible too); at large $a$ lensing is dominant, with ellipsoidal variation and Doppler boosting tending to zero as $a$ increases \citep{sorabella_modeling_2022}.

These effects all depend on the orbital phase at a given time during the simulation. The zero point for this is defined as the moment of maximum lensing magnification (i.e. the time of minimum projected separation between source and lens, $t_0$). The periapsis phase angle is defined in terms of this zero point; i.e. the phase angle at which periastron passage occurs, relative to the position at $t_0$. Note that this is different from the usual convention of measuring orbital phase relative to periastron passage and was chosen to simplify the computation of the lensing effect.

The source-lens distance is calculated according to the phase angle a given time, meaning the source-lens distance changes during the microlensing event according to the eccentricity. Strictly speaking, the Einstein ring radius should also change as a function of orbital phase, but this effect is expected to be small and so is not accounted for in the present work. Instead, the radius at $t_0$ is used for all calculations.

All simulated light curves are 27 days long (the duration of a TESS sector). The peak time, $t_0$, is randomly selected to fall somewhere in the 27 day time window.

We use the Einstein radius and impact-parameter convention of \cite{wiktorowicz_predicting_2021}, generalised from the circular-orbit case by replacing the orbital separation with the instantaneous Keplerian lens-source separation,
\begin{equation}
    R_\mathrm{E} = \left(\frac{4 G M_\mathrm{lens} r(t_0) \sin i}{c^2}\right)^{1/2},
    \quad
    b = \frac{a |\cos i|}{R_\mathrm{E}},
    \quad
    b_\mathrm{max} = 1 + \frac{R_\star}{R_\mathrm{E}},
\end{equation}
where $r(t_0)$ is the lens-source separation and $\omega$ is the phase angle of periapsis relative to $t_0$. For a circular orbit, $r(t_0)=a$ and this reduces to the expression used by \cite{wiktorowicz_predicting_2021}. We define the orbital phase relative to $t_0$ as the true orbital longitude, using the true anomaly $\nu(t)$,
\begin{equation}
    \phi(t) = \omega + \nu(t),
\end{equation}
where $\nu(t)$ is measured relative to periapsis. The true anomaly is obtained from Kepler's equation using the mean anomaly $\mathcal{M}$ and eccentric anomaly $E$,
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{M}(t) & = \mathcal{M}_0 + 2\pi\frac{t - t_0}{P_\mathrm{orb}}, \\
    \mathcal{M}(t) & = E(t) - e\sin E(t),                                  \\
    \nu(t)         & = 2\arctan2\left[
        \sqrt{1+e}\sin\frac{E(t)}{2},
        \sqrt{1-e}\cos\frac{E(t)}{2}
        \right],                                                           \\
    P_\mathrm{orb} & =
    2\pi\left(\frac{a^3}{G(M_\mathrm{lens} + M_\star)}\right)^{1/2},
\end{align}
with $\mathcal{M}_0$ chosen such that $\phi(t_0)=0$. $\arctan2$ is the standard quadrant-aware inverse tangent. The true anomaly at the lensing peak is $\nu_0=-\omega$, so the corresponding eccentric anomaly and mean anomaly are
\begin{align}
    E_0           & = 2\arctan2\left[
        \sqrt{1-e}\sin\frac{\nu_0}{2},
        \sqrt{1+e}\cos\frac{\nu_0}{2}
        \right],                        \\
    \mathcal{M}_0 & = E_0 - e\sin E_0 .
\end{align}
For this orbital phase, the true orbital lens-source separation is
\begin{equation}
    r[\phi(t)] =
    \frac{a(1 - e^2)}
    {1 + e\cos[\phi(t) - \omega]}.
\end{equation}
The projected lens-source separation in Einstein-radius units is
\begin{equation}
    u(t) = \frac{1}{R_\mathrm{E}}
    \left[
        \left(r[\phi(t)] \sin\phi(t)\right)^2
        + \left(b R_\mathrm{E}\right)^2
        \right]^{1/2},
\end{equation}
where $u(t)$ is dimensionless. The self-lensing magnification is calculated with the integrated Witt--Mao finite-source formalism \citep{witt_can_1994}. The implementation evaluates the closed-form uniform-disc magnification,
\begin{equation}
    A_\mathrm{WM}(u, \rho_\star) =
    \frac{
        C_E E(m) + C_K K(m) + C_\Pi \Pi(n, m)
    }{\pi},
\end{equation}
where $K$, $E$, and $\Pi$ are complete elliptic integrals of the first, second, and third kind. The coefficients $C_E$, $C_K$, and $C_\Pi$, and the elliptic-integral arguments $m$ and $n$, are functions only of the projected lens-source separation and $\rho_\star = R_\star / R_\mathrm{E}$. The disc-integrated magnification used in the simulated light curves is then evaluated by quadrature over a linearly limb-darkened stellar disc,
\begin{align}
    A_\mathrm{SL}(t) & =
    \frac{
        \int_0^1
        A_\mathrm{WM}\left[u(t), \rho\rho_\star\right]
        I(\mu)\,2\rho\,\mathrm{d}\rho
    }{
        \int_0^1 I(\mu)\,2\rho\,\mathrm{d}\rho
    },
    \nonumber                             \\
    \mu              & = \sqrt{1-\rho^2},
    \quad
    I(\mu) = 1 - \gamma(1-\mu),
\end{align}
where $\gamma=0.6$ \citep{claret_limb_2017} is the linear limb-darkening coefficient, which is suitable for Sun-like stars observed by TESS \textbf{TODO: Defend this choice or choose a more representative value}.

Calculation of ellipsoidal variations and Doppler boosting are adapted from \cite{sorabella_modeling_2022}, with the orbital separation and radial velocity generalised to the eccentric case. The ellipsoidal-variation term is
\begin{align}
    A_\mathrm{EV}(t) & =
    -\beta
    \frac{M_\mathrm{lens}}{M_\star}
    \left(\frac{R_\star}{r[\phi(t)]}\right)^3
    \sin^2 i \cos 2\phi(t),
    \nonumber                                                           \\
    \beta            & = 0.15\,\frac{(1 + g)(15 + \gamma)}{3 - \gamma},
\end{align}
where $\beta$ is the ellipsoidal-variation coefficient and $g=0.32$ is the gravity-darkening coefficient for a Sun-like star \citep{lucy_gravity-darkening_1967}.

The Doppler-boosting term is
\begin{equation}
    A_\mathrm{DB}(t) =
    (3 - \alpha)\frac{v_r(t)}{c},
    \quad
    \alpha =
    \frac{\exp(x)(3 - x) - 3}{\exp(x) - 1},
    \quad
    x = \frac{h\nu_\mathrm{TESS}}{k_\mathrm{B}T_\mathrm{eff}},
\end{equation}
where $\nu_\mathrm{TESS}$ is the TESS-band effective frequency and $v_r(t)$ is the source radial velocity. The combined model flux is
\begin{equation}
    F(t) = F_0 A_\mathrm{SL}(t)\left[1 + A_\mathrm{EV}(t) + A_\mathrm{DB}(t)\right].
\end{equation}

\subsection{Stellar noise} \label{sec:gp}

% Adapted from Maddie's thesis

Gaussian processes are designed to model smoothly varying correlated noise \citep{rasmussen_gaussian_2005}. In the exoplanet community, this has been highly successful in allowing exoplanet signals to be disentangled from stellar noise. In order to generate realistic, TESS-like light curves, we combine several kernels to model granulation and asteroseismic modes, based on the simulated stellar properties. We use \texttt{tinygp} with quasi-separable simple harmonic oscillator (SHO) kernels \textbf{TODO: Citation for tinygp?}.

Granulation is a form of aperiodic correlated noise due to convection cells at the stellar surface. Granulation appears as low-amplitude correlated noise; aperiodic in nature, it prefers certain timescales dictated by the size and age of the star \citep{kjeldsen_amplitudes_1995}. As derived
in \cite{foreman-mackey_exoplanet_2021}, we consider this a characteristic timescale of a damped stochastic process. We model such a process using a damped harmonic oscillator kernel with a fixed quality factor ($Q= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$) parameter to create a damped random walk (DRW). This creates frequency-dependent broadband noise with a characteristic timescale but no dominant frequencies. The characteristic
timescale in the model $\tau$ then reflects the average lifetime of a single convective cell, while the amplitude scales with the number of granules contributing to the observable flux and the source-star surface gravity, $g_\star$ \citep{kjeldsen_amplitudes_1995,pereira_gaussian_2019,foreman-mackey_exoplanet_2021}. The granulation timescale,
$\tau_\mathrm{gran}$, for a source star of mass $M_\star$ and radius $R_\star$, goes as

\begin{equation}
    \tau_\mathrm{gran} \propto \frac{1}{g_\star}
    \propto \left(\frac{R_\star^2}{M_\star}\right).
\end{equation}

This model captures the temporal decorrelation the granulation component light curve over time, appropriate for granulation, where surface features evolve on a finite timescale and exhibit only short-range temporal correlations \citep{aigrain_gaussian_2023}. This model has also been tested and applied in previous GP transit studies \citep{brewer_gaussian_2009,nicholson_quasi-periodic_2022}. Overall, the DRW kernel effectively models granulation noise without requiring high model complexity or prior knowledge of the star’s convective dynamics.

Asteroseismic oscillations are caused by the propagation of sound waves through the star's outer convective zone. \citep{kjeldsen_amplitudes_1995,chaplin_asteroseismology_2013}. These occur on the timescales of minutes to tens of hours, placing them directly into the observing window of light curves from instruments such as TESS. Again, we choose a damped harmonic oscillator kernel, but this time with a free quality factor to allow for the presence of a periodic component \citep{brewer_gaussian_2009,foreman-mackey_exoplanet_2021}. The frequency of the primary mode, given by the $\nu_\mathrm{seis}$ hyper-parameter, is proportional to the source-star surface gravity, $g_\star$, and the effective temperature, $T_\mathrm{eff}$, and therefore depends
on the stellar properties of the simulated system.

\begin{equation}
    \nu_\mathrm{seis} \propto \frac{g_\star}{\sqrt{T_\mathrm{eff}}}
    \propto \frac{M_\star}{R_\star^2 \sqrt{T_\mathrm{eff}}},
\end{equation}
where $M_\star$, $R_\star$, and $T_\mathrm{eff}$ are the mass, radius, and effective
temperature of the source star.

In the GP implementation, these frequency scalings are evaluated from internally
consistent stellar relations rather than fitted independently for every light curve.
For a simulated stellar mass $M_\star$, we estimate the stellar radius and effective
temperature from main-sequence scaling relations. Let $\mathcal{R}$ be the dimensionless
mass-radius relation, $L(M_\star)$ the corresponding mass-luminosity relation, and
$\sigma_\mathrm{SB}$ the Stefan--Boltzmann constant; $M_\odot$ and $R_\odot$
are the solar mass and radius. Then
\begin{equation}
    R_\star = R_\odot \mathcal{R}(M_\star/M_\odot),
    \qquad
    T_\mathrm{eff} =
    \left(\frac{L(M_\star)}{4\pi \sigma_\mathrm{SB} R_\star^2}\right)^{1/4}.
\end{equation}
The asteroseismic frequency is then initialised using the usual
$\nu_\mathrm{max}$ scaling relation. Let $\nu_{\mathrm{max},\odot}$ be the
solar value of $\nu_\mathrm{max}$, and let $T_\odot$ be the solar effective
temperature. Then
\begin{equation}
    \nu_\mathrm{seis}
    =
    \nu_{\mathrm{max},\odot}
    \left(\frac{M_\star}{M_\odot}\right)
    \left(\frac{R_\star}{R_\odot}\right)^{-2}
    \left(\frac{T_\mathrm{eff}}{T_\odot}\right)^{-1/2},
\end{equation}
The granulation characteristic frequency is taken to be proportional to this value, adopting values around an empirical proportionality constant calibrated by \citep{kallinger_connection_2014},
\begin{equation}
    \nu_\mathrm{gran} = ~0.30\,\nu_\mathrm{seis}.
\end{equation}
The kernel hyperparameters are stored as $\log_{10}\nu$ in Hz and converted to angular frequency internally when constructing the SHO kernels.

Kernel amplitudes are set in count-rate units by scaling the fractional stellar-variability amplitudes with the source's expected TESS count rate. If
$N_\mathrm{TESS}$ is the expected number of detected source counts per cadence, the standard
deviation of GP component $q$ is initialised as
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_q = f_q N_\mathrm{TESS},
\end{equation}
% TODO: The current generator uses the geometric midpoint of each stated ppm band, rather than drawing an amplitude within the band; update the text below if this remains the intended implementation.
where $f_q$ is a representative fractional variability amplitude. In the simulations we use broad ppm-level bands for these fractional amplitudes, with granulation initialised between 50--300 ppm and asteroseismic variability between 100--1000 ppm.

The same apparent-magnitude calculation is also used to set the TESS white-noise level. The TESS-band source magnitude, $m_\mathrm{TESS}$, is converted to $N_\mathrm{TESS}$, and an empirical TESS noise model provides a fractional per-cadence uncertainty $\eta(m_\mathrm{TESS})$. For the GP covariance matrix $\Sigma_{ij}$ and the Kronecker delta $\delta_{ij}$, this gives a TESS white-noise standard deviation $\sigma_\mathrm{TESS}$ and diagonal covariance contribution
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_\mathrm{TESS} = \eta(m_\mathrm{TESS})N_\mathrm{TESS},
    \qquad
    \Sigma_{ij} \rightarrow \Sigma_{ij} + \sigma_\mathrm{TESS}^2 \delta_{ij}.
\end{equation}
This term is fixed by the simulated source brightness rather than fitted as an additional free GP jitter parameter.

However, such variations are often overpowered by more global variations, such as asteroseismic modes for distant stars, where surface structure cannot be resolved. We therefore assume that the jitter term is sufficient to account for magnetic contributions to the underlying stellar noise.

\subsection{Photometric MCMC fit} \label{sec:phot-fit}

Each simulated photometric light curve is fitted via MCMC using a random-walk Metropolis-Hastings sampler implemented with the BlackJAX package (128 walkers, 1,000 total steps, including 200 burn-in steps). For these fits it is assumed we know the stellar physical parameters exactly, along with the relative amplification (Section~\ref{sec:phot-sims}) and crossing time. In reality there will be uncertainty in these values, but determining them is a separate problem which is out of scope for the present work. Light curves are binned according to their crossing time to minimise computation time while preserving the shape of the self-lensing peak (Table~\ref{tab:photometric-binning}).

    \label{tab:photometric-binning}
    \begin{tabular}{ll}
        \hline
        Effective crossing time, $\tau_\mathrm{eff}$ & Photometric bin width \\
        \hline
        $\tau_\mathrm{eff} < 4$ h & 10 min (native cadence) \\
        $4$ h $\leq \tau_\mathrm{eff} \leq 1$ d & 1 h \\
        $\tau_\mathrm{eff} > 1$ d & 6 h \\
        \hline

For computational efficiency, fits are run as batched, vectorised fits on a GPU. We fit only the fundamental physical parameters of the system,
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\theta} = (a, i, M_\mathrm{lens}, e, \omega),
\end{equation}
with the stellar mass, stellar radius, effective temperature, distance, and lensing-peak time held fixed at their simulated values. Orbital period, impact parameter, maximum possible impact parameter, and effective lensing-event duration are derived from the fitted physical parameters rather than fitted directly.

Walkers are initialised using an estimated parameter distribution calculated from $\tau_\mathrm{eff}$ and $A_\mathrm{SL}$. Similar to Figure~\ref{fig:self-lensing-observable-space}, parameters are calculated in the region around the observed values, and then these are clustered by the parameters in $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ using $k$-means clustering to find distinct groupings of estimated parameters. The central point of each cluster is used to choose initial walker positions. This ensures the random walk thoroughly explores the area of the parameter space with the highest likelihood given the known amplification and crossing time, without constraining it from exploring beyond those regions.

We use a uniform prior within the corresponding parameter limits in Table~\ref{tab:phot_sim_parameter_ranges}, with negative infinity probability outside these limits. We also require trial systems to satisfy the self-lensing geometry condition $b \leq b_\mathrm{max}$.

At the end of the MCMC run, the posterior parameter distribution is clustered using $k$-means clustering to derive a set of distinct solutions which could produce the simulated light curve (to account for degeneracies).

\subsection{Radial velocity calculation} \label{sec:rv-sims}

The RV simulations use the same physical parameters and phase convention as the photometric simulations. Let $\mu_g=G(M_\mathrm{lens}+M_\star)$ be the standard gravitational parameter, and $h=[\mu_g a(1-e^2)]^{1/2}$ be the specific angular momentum of the relative orbit. The source-star barycentric radial and tangential orbital-velocity components are
\begin{align}
    v_{\mathrm{rad},\star}(t) & =
    \frac{M_\mathrm{lens}}{M_\mathrm{lens}+M_\star}
    \frac{\mu_g}{h}
    e\sin\nu(t),
    \nonumber                     \\
    v_{\mathrm{tan},\star}(t) & =
    \frac{M_\mathrm{lens}}{M_\mathrm{lens}+M_\star}
    \frac{\mu_g}{h}
    \left[1 + e\cos\nu(t)\right].
\end{align}
The simulated line-of-sight radial velocity is then
\begin{equation}
    v_r(t) =
    \left[
        v_{\mathrm{tan},\star}(t)\sin\phi(t)
        -
        v_{\mathrm{rad},\star}(t)\cos\phi(t)
        \right]\sin i.
\end{equation}
This reduces to the circular-orbit convention $v_r(t)\propto\sin\phi(t)$ when $e=0$, but for eccentric orbits the radial component means the RV at $t_0$ is not generally zero.

We add varying amounts of instrumental noise ($\sigma_\mathrm{ins} \in \{0.1, 1, 10, 100, 1000\}$ m/s), and an additional fixed Gaussian noise ($\sigma_\star = 10$ m/s) is added to represent stellar noise. For simplicity, we do not include proper motion or heliocentric motion, as these would be corrected before fitting real observations.

\subsection{Targeted radial velocity scheduling} \label{sec:scheduling}

% Mention that we do the prefit, detect multi-mode (degenerate) solutions, and account for that in scheduling. We use half of the available observations to deliberately distinguish modes based on their predicted RVs, then the rest of maximise phase coverage by minimising gaps.

The simulated RV curves start at randomised times between 1 year and 10 years from the end of the photometry. This is to simulate a realistic delay in identifying candidates in the photometry and organising follow-up of those candidates. For this work we assume each observing campaign will last five years, and we trial varying numbers of RV observations, $3 \leq N_\mathrm{obs} \leq 10$. We do not attempt to simulate fully realistic observing (with observing seasons, seeing, varying weather conditions, etc.) as we are mainly interested in measuring the effects of instrument selection and number of observations.

The distinct solutions derived in Section~\ref{sec:phot-fit} are used to compute a schedule for the radial velocity observations, in which observations are deliberately timed to distinguish between the solutions and maximise the information gained about the binary orbit.

First, in the event that the photometric fit only found one plausible solution, the orbital period of that solution is checked to determine whether a lensing event will occur during the simulated observing window. If so, three observations are scheduled around this event, one at $t_0$, and two at $t_0 \pm \delta_t$. $\delta_t$ is chosen by drawing random samples from the photometric MCMC posterior and choosing the smallest value of $\delta_t$ where the change in RV is larger than $5 \sigma_\mathrm{ins}$ for $>90\%$ of samples, assuming $\omega = \pi$; i.e. the smallest time offset where a change in RV can be reliably measured by the simulated spectrograph. This triplet of observations provides information about $e$ and $\omega$: if $v_r(t_0) \neq 0$, then $e > 0$; if $v_r(t_0) = 0$, then either $e = 0$ or $\omega = 0$ (or both) and the value of $v_r(t_0 \pm \delta_t)$ provides information about these values.

If there are multiple plausible photometric MCMC solutions, the predicted RV values for each of them over the observing campaign are calculated, and the difference in RV values for each pair of solutions is calculated (each ``solution" is a cluster of posterior samples, so the separation is actually the squared difference between the mean RVs of each cluster, normalised by the error). The final set of observation times is chosen by iteratively selecting the times which maximise the minimum separation between solutions, for a minimum of three observations and a maximum of $\lceil N_\mathrm{obs}/2 \rceil$.

Any remaining observations are scheduled to minimise the gaps between observations. For a circular orbit, this is equivalent to limiting the gaps in orbital phase between observations, maximising the orbital phase coverage (within the constraint of the length of the observing campaign), and minimising the variances in fitted model parameters later derived from the observations \citep{koen_how_2024}.

\subsection{Joint photometric-RV MCMC fit} \label{sec:fitting}

Each simulated pair of photometric light curves and RV curves is then fitted via MCMC using the same setup as in Section~\ref{sec:phot-fit}, using the final walker state from the photometric fit as the initial state for the joint fit. Photometry is binned as per the first fit, while RV data are not binned. The Metropolis-Hastings random-walk proposal scale is reduced based on the number of RV measurements, when RV data are present, or otherwise on the number of photometric points, to allow the walkers to efficiently explore the narrower posterior where more data are present.

For a trial parameter vector $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, let $v_\mathrm{mod}(t_k;\boldsymbol{\theta})$ be the model RV at the $k$th RV observation time, and let $N_\mathrm{RV}$ be the number of RV measurements. The RV contribution to the fit statistic is
\begin{equation}
    Q_\mathrm{RV}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) =
    -\sum_{k=1}^{N_\mathrm{RV}}
    \left[
        \frac{v_k - v_\mathrm{mod}(t_k;\boldsymbol{\theta})}
        {\sigma_{\mathrm{RV},k}}
        \right]^2,
\end{equation}
where $v_k$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{RV},k}$ are the simulated RV measurement and uncertainty. For the light curve, let $f_j$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{LC},j}$ be the binned photometric flux and uncertainty at the $j$th binned time, let $f_\mathrm{mod}(t_j;\boldsymbol{\theta})$ be the corresponding model flux, and let $N_\mathrm{LC}$ be the number of fitted light-curve bins. The light-curve contribution is
\begin{equation}
    Q_\mathrm{LC}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) =
    -\sum_{j=1}^{N_\mathrm{LC}}
    \left[
        \frac{f_j - f_\mathrm{mod}(t_j;\boldsymbol{\theta})}
        {\sigma_{\mathrm{LC},j}}
        \right]^2.
\end{equation}
The quantity sampled by the MCMC is then the sum of these terms plus the parameter-prior term, $\ln \pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})$,
\begin{equation}
    \ln p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\mathrm{data}) =
    \ln \pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}) +
    Q_\mathrm{RV}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) +
    Q_\mathrm{LC}(\boldsymbol{\theta}).
\end{equation}
The reported fitted parameters are posterior summaries rather than single maximum-posterior samples: after burn-in, we retain the 256 samples with the highest finite posterior values for each fit and take the mean of the fitted parameters, using a circular mean for the periapsis phase angle.

\subsection{Predicting recovery of real self-lensing systems} \label{sec:pop-synth}

In order to inform expectations about how many self-lensing systems might be correctly characterised as a result of detection in modern photometric surveys, we seek to combine our estimates of the recoverability of the physical properties of self-lensing systems with the detectability estimates of \cite{wiktorowicz_predicting_2021}. We note that the survey-specific detection yields we use here come from their Table~2, which is an optimistic upper limit including events for which only one photometric detection is made (i.e. one photometric point in the light curve falls within the lensing event). In reality, detections would require multiple photometric points to estimate $t_0$ and $\tau_\mathrm{eff}$.

One important difference between \cite{wiktorowicz_predicting_2021} and the present work is that they use population synthesis to generate a physically plausible distribution of physical properties, as the basis for simulating the total number of detectable systems observed by each photometric survey. In the present work, we do not simulate a physically representative distribution of parameters, because our primary aim is to explore the ability to recover those parameters regardless of how common a given configuration will be. In other words, we want to simulate very rare configurations as thoroughly as more common ones. We are also concerned only with characterisation of lenses/recovery of physical parameters via spectroscopic RV measurements after detection in photometry, while \cite{wiktorowicz_predicting_2021} investigates photometric detectability.

Still, it is useful to derive from our simulations an estimate of how many systems we should expect to be properly characterised, assuming the predicted number of photometric detections is made in each survey. To this end we build a nearest-neighbour emulator ($k=25$) to map the population synthesis results onto the fitting/recovery results for our radial velocity simulations. For each population-synthesis system and each RV simulation we define a scaled feature vector
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x} =
    \left(
    \frac{\log_{10} M_\mathrm{lens} - \mu_M}{s_M},
    \frac{\log_{10} P_\mathrm{orb} - \mu_P}{s_P},
    \frac{e - \mu_e}{s_e},
    \frac{b - \mu_b}{s_b}
    \right),
\end{equation}
where the centring constants $\mu$ are medians and the scale factors $s$ are the 16th--84th percentile ranges, giving a robust measure of the central spread of each coordinate for each respective parameter. Coordinates are normalised by centring and scaling, to prevent the nearest-neighbour search from being dominated by those with the largest numerical spread. For a population-synthesis system $j$, the emulator identifies the set $\mathcal{N}_k(j)$ of the $k$ nearest RV simulations in this scaled space and assigns inverse-distance weights
\begin{equation}
    w_{ij} =
    \frac{(d_{ij} + \epsilon)^{-1}}
    {\sum_{\ell \in \mathcal{N}_k(j)} (d_{\ell j} + \epsilon)^{-1}},
    \quad i \in \mathcal{N}_k(j),
\end{equation}
where $d_{ij} = \lVert\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j\rVert$ and $\epsilon = 10^{-12}$ is a small numerical constant to avoid division by zero. For each observing strategy and recovery metric, the emulated recovery probability is then
\begin{equation}
    \hat{p}_{j} =
    \frac{\sum_{i \in \mathcal{N}_k(j)} v_i w_{ij} q_i}
    {\sum_{i \in \mathcal{N}_k(j)} v_i w_{ij}},
    \qquad
    C_j = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{N}_k(j)} v_i w_{ij},
\end{equation}
where $q_i$ is the measured RV outcome and $v_i$ indicates whether that neighbouring simulation has a valid fit for the relevant strategy. The associated coverage $C_j$ is therefore the fraction of the neighbour weight supported by valid fitted simulations. We emulate the detected-population outcomes in this way: correct compact-object class, lens-mass recovery, and orbital-period recovery. The final fractions for the detected population-synthesis sample are obtained by averaging these emulated probabilities using the population weights $W_j$,
\begin{equation}
    \bar{p} = \frac{\sum_j W_j \hat{p}_j}{\sum_j W_j}.
\end{equation}

We adopt the following mass bins and object classes: $<3 \text{ M}_\odot$ (neutron stars); $3-5 \text{ M}_\odot$ (lower mass gap); $5-20 \text{ M}_\odot$ (lower mass black holes); $20-50 \text{ M}_\odot$ (higher mass black holes; separated from the lower mass to assess how black hole mass affects recovery). We caveat this analysis by noting that our simulations include relatively fewer low mass lenses than high mass lenses, however these results are still informative about our ability to recover masses in these ranges.

It is worth noting that these results represent an ideal best case scenario for obtaining follow-up observations. We aim to assess a representative range of observing schedules, rather than rigorously simulating realistic observations. We do not account for observing seasons, we assume every target is always observable, we do not simulate weather or bad seeing, and we assume only a simple model of noise in the RV measurements (varying amounts of Gaussian noise to simulate spectrograph accuracy, ranging from sub-metre per second accuracy up to kilometre per second accuracy, to represent the range of spectrograph facilities currently available), plus a fixed 10 m/s Gaussian stellar noise). We also assume the exact time of the lensing peak ($t_0$) and the properties of the stellar companion (mass, radius, effective temperature, and distance from Earth) are known with no uncertainty; in practice these would all be subject to some amount of uncertainty.

\subsection{Recovering object class}
While the ultimate goal of our observing campaign will be to accurately measure the lens mass and other physical properties of the population of self-lensing systems, we anticipate having to grapple with many orders of magnitude more false positives than true self-lensing systems. This situation will therefore require an informed, systematic way of triaging and prioritising targets for follow-up. The volume of false positives is anticipated to be so large that we will want to obtain as few observations of each target as possible in order to characterise what type of compact object the lens is (or, indeed, whether each target is even a self-lensing system to begin with). In the present work we assume every target is \textit{some} kind of self-lensing system, but even with that assumption, some classes of object will be more astrophysically interesting than others, and some will require more or fewer observations in order to confirm. It is therefore informative to know whether we can accurately predict the \textit{class} of the lens, even in cases where we can not accurately predict the mass. Being able to do so will allow us to allocate resources accordingly when we plan follow-up observations.

Figure~\ref{fig:lens-mass-class-recovery-by-lens-mass} shows how well the object class is recovered as a function of the observing strategy. A system with a fitted mass over 5 solar masses can often be identified as a black hole even when its fitted mass has a large error. Similarly, those with a fitted mass < 3M$_\odot$ can be reasonably assumed to be white dwarfs or neutron stars. This can inform our priorities when planning observations; e.g. we can choose to obtain additional observations of targets with fitted masses that fall in the 3--5 M$_\odot$ the mass gap, or we can treat higher mass systems as confirmed with fewer observations. We find that the number and timing of observations mostly have only a small effect on the recovery of the object class, while RV accuracy is more important for lower mass systems but not for high mass systems. This suggests a staged observing strategy would be appropriate, where lower accuracy observations are first obtained for all targets, with higher accuracy observations reserved for those which seem to be lower mass. \subsection{Orbital period relative error}

The orbital period strongly influences the shape of the RV curve. In the circular-orbit limit the RV is zero at the lensing event and reaches an extremum half an orbit later; for the eccentric systems fitted here, eccentricity and periapsis phase shift the detailed RV shape and extrema. When we can fix the orbital phase at a specific time (as we can when lensing is observed), obtaining an RV curve, even over a partial orbit, can tightly constrain the orbital period, eccentricity, and periapsis phase angle even for very long orbits.

Figure~\ref{fig:orbital-period-relative-error-by-period} shows the median relative error in orbital period as a function of the observing strategy, RV accuracy, and orbital period. In all cases, the 2 day--2.5 year orbital period range has larger relative errors than the 2.5--18 year range. This suggests the observations need to be frequent enough to repeatedly sample a single orbit, rather than sampling multiple orbits (\textbf{TODO: See if this is still true once the final figures are in}).

RV accuracy also has a measurable effect on the median relative error, but this varies by orbital period, suggesting that in many cases, even kilometre per second scale accuracy is sufficient to constrain the parameters, and improving this further does not always reduce the median relative error. However, for longer orbital periods the relative error becomes more sensitive to RV accuracy. This is due to the smaller rate of change of the RV in these systems and a more accurate spectrograph is required to detect the relatively small change in RV over the course of a few years when the orbital period is decades or centuries long. With sufficiently accurate RV measurements, we can characterise self-lensing systems with orbital periods of many centuries.

We can save a small amount of computational effort by independently fitting the RV, but to avoid needing a larger number of RV measurements we still require a photometric detection first.

\section{Conclusions}
We know self-lensing systems can be confirmed from photometry alone, and when this is not possible they can be confirmed with RV, at least in the case of relatively short period white dwarf lenses. We show it is possible to characterise lenses across the full compact object mass range, including for very long orbital periods where only one self-lensing event is detectable.

We show that while measuring the lens mass can be challenging, with only a small number of RV measurements it is possible to determine the broad compact object class. This can then inform prioritisation of further observations. With this in mind, our recommendation for following up on photometrically-detected self-lensing candidates is a staged follow-up strategy, where all candidates are observed a small number of times to determine which ones are truly binaries and what the lens class is in those cases. A second stage of follow-up observations can then concentrate on the true binaries to ascertain the lens mass to a higher precision.

We show that the highest mass lenses tend to have the lowest relative errors, even with large orbital periods. We also show that the number and timing of observations matter more than RV precision in many cases. This is helpful when it comes to planning follow-up observations, as it widens the pool of facilities which can provide useful RV measurements. It is also promising that we are able to constrain the simulation parameters even for very long period systems. This means it is worthwhile following up on single-event detections as well as those which are seen to be periodic.

Combining parameter recovery rates with previous population synthesis results suggests that it is possible to confirm the existence of a substantial number of self-lensing systems in surveys such as TESS, ZTF, and LSST. The estimates presented here are optimistic upper limits for confirmed detections under ideal conditions, but bode well for our ability to find self-lensing systems in these and other surveys.
