\section{Introduction}
Self-lensing systems are binary systems which are close to being edge-on, consisting of a star in orbit with a compact object. With an appropriate separation distance, the Einstein radius of the compact object can be larger than its surface radius, and if the orbital inclination is close enough to edge-on this can result in a measurable microlensing amplification of the stellar flux once per orbit.

To date, five self-lensing binaries have been confirmed, each containing a white dwarf-stellar binary, all of which have relatively close, short-period orbits. However, it is expected that stellar-compact object binaries should span the full range of compact object masses, as well as the full range of orbital configurations.

Photometry remains the best way to detect self-lensing binaries, and previous works have assessed the detectability of self-lensing binaries in various photometric surveys \citep{wiktorowicz_predicting_2021}. These results look promising, with modern all-sky surveys expected to imminently uncover a population of compact objects in self-lensing binaries, including the currently under-studied population of quiescent black holes.

While detection of self-lensing systems with photometry alone has been demonstrated, modelling of such systems and characterisation of the compact component is subject to several degeneracies between the physical parameters which can make it challenging to accurately determine the mass of the lens. For short-period binaries it should be possible to derive a photometric RV by modelling Doppler boosting (\textbf{TODO: }CITATIONS, including COBALT-BLUE), but for systems with no detectable Doppler boosting effect, spectroscopic observations are the best way to solve the binary's physical parameters. This is especially true for systems with the longest orbital periods, for which we may only be able to observe a single lensing event.

In this work, we therefore consider the question of how many spectroscopic RV measurements are required, and at what level of precision, in order to accurately model the self-lensing system parameters, as a function of those parameters, including systems with orbital periods on the order of centuries for which the orbital period can not be determined photometrically. This will inform observing strategies for follow-up of the large number of candidates expected to come from new surveys, allowing the most efficient use of resources by not obtaining more observations than necessary. We also consider the usefulness of photometric-only fitting to guide RV observing strategy.

We will show that it is possible to accurately model very long period self-lensing binaries with a handful of RV measurements. In many cases, photometric-only fits can come close to determining the properties, so that we don't need many RV measurements to fit the parameters accurately.

The main distinguishing feature of self-lensing vs microlensing is the periodicity of the lensing flares. To claim photometric discovery of a self-lensing system, ideally we would aim to detect this periodicity. We show that self-lensing can be confirmed even when periodicity can not be observed.

\section{Methods}

The aim of this work is to quantity the observational requirements for radial velocity (RV) measurements that will allow us to determine or confirm the lens mass and orbital configuration of self-lensing binary candidates discovered via our ongoing photometric search. We investigate the relationship between observing cadence, observing campaign duration (baseline), and accuracy of RV measurements, and the rate of recovery of physical parameters as a function of lens mass. We investigate recovery rate by both accurate lens mass (determined to within 10\% of the true value) and broad compact object class (i.e. distinguishing black holes from other compact objects even if the recovered mass deviates from the true mass by more than 10\%).

As the basis of this work, we have generated a library of self-lensing photometric simulations. This was generated for a range of physical parameters, filtered to include only the simulated light curves which contain visible lensing peaks, where the peak amplitude is larger than the photometric noise. We also exclude systems overflowing their Roche lobe and any systems where lensing is not geometrically possible. These simulations are described in Section~\ref{sec:phot-sims}.

The radial velocity measurements are simulated for the same set of simulation parameters as the photometry (Section~\ref{sec:rv-sims}). The RV simulations are then sampled according to a range of observing schedules, and Gaussian noise is added to simulate a range of spectrograph accuracies (Section~\ref{sec:rv-realistic}). An MCMC fit is then performed to find the self-lensing physical parameters (the lens mass, semi-major axis, inclination angle, eccentricity, and periapsis phase angle; we assume the stellar properties, mass, radius, effective temperature, and distance from Earth, are known) which jointly best fit the photometry and RV (Section~\ref{sec:fitting}). We then aggregate the recovery rates across observing parameters and self-lensing physical parameters to investigate the effect of each parameter on the recovery rate.

Finally, we map the recovery rates onto previous population synthesis results to estimate the numbers of self-lensing systems which can be confirmed following detection in various photometric surveys (Section~\ref{sec:pop-synth}).

\subsection{Photometric simulations} \label{sec:phot-sims}
We note that the photometric model described here is similar to the recently published COBALT-BLUE model \citep{...}, but uses a simpler approximation to the full photometric forward model. Compared with COBALT-BLUE, our model adopts a local approximation to the eccentric self-lensing geometry and lower-order treatments of Doppler boosting and ellipsoidal variability, while adding the spectroscopic radial-velocity component needed to study the recovery of lens mass and other physical parameters. We anticipate combining the two models in a future work.

These simulations comprise a sample of self-lensing system parameters which is physically complete (i.e. covering the full plausible range of physical parameters) but not physically realistic (i.e. parameters are not sampled according to the frequency they are expected to occur in nature). This is ideal as the basis for determining the ranges of parameters which produce self-lensing effects which are detectable, independent of how common or uncommon particular parameter ranges are expected to be.

The photometric simulations comprise realistic simulated TESS light curves (including stellar noise generated with a Gaussian process) for each sample self-lensing system. When generating the simulations, the parameter values were sampled uniformly between the limits given in Table~\ref{tab:phot_sim_parameter_ranges}, with the exception of semi-major axis and eccentricity which are sampled on a log scale to increase the number of samples at smaller values (the light curve morphology changes roughly in proportion to the relative change in these values; e.g. a 5 AU change in semi-major axis has a much larger effect going from 1 AU to 6 AU than for 100 AU to 105 AU).

\begin{table}
    \centering
    \caption{Parameter ranges used to generate the photometric simulation library.}
    \label{tab:phot_sim_parameter_ranges}
    \begin{tabular}{lll}
        \hline
        Parameter         & Description           & Range                            \\
        \hline
        $a$               & Semi-major axis       & 0.1--200 AU                      \\
        $i$               & Orbital inclination   & $0.5\pi - 10^{-4}$--$0.5\pi$ rad \\
        $M_\mathrm{lens}$ & Lens mass             & 0.5--50 M$_\odot$                \\
        $M_\star$         & Stellar mass          & 0.5--50 M$_\odot$                \\
        $e$               & Eccentricity          & 0--0.999999                      \\
        $\omega$          & Periapsis phase angle & 0--$2\pi$                        \\
        $d$               & Distance from Earth   & 0.1--100 kpc                     \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Three photometric effects are included when generating the light curves: self-lensing, ellipsoidal variations, and Doppler boosting. At small values of $a$ the light curve is dominated by ellipsoidal variations and Doppler boosting (though lensing is often visible too); at large $a$ lensing is dominant, with ellipsoidal variation and Doppler boosting tending to zero as $a$ increases \citep{sorabella_modeling_2022}.

These effects all depend on the orbital phase at a given time during the simulation. The zero point for this is defined as the moment of maximum lensing magnification (i.e. the time of minimum projected separation between source and lens, $t_0$). The periapsis phase angle is defined in terms of this zero point; i.e. the phase angle at which periastron passage occurs, relative to the position at $t_0$. Note that this is different from the usual convention of measuring orbital phase relative to periastron passage; this was chosen to simplify the computation of the lensing effect, as the time of the lensing peak is used as the basis of detections when we conduct searches of real photometric data.

The source-lens distance is calculated for each time according to the phase angle at that time, meaning the source-lens distance changes during the microlensing event according to the eccentricity. Strictly speaking, the Einstein ring radius should also change as a function of orbital phase, but this effect is expected to be small and so is not accounted for in the present work. Instead, the radius at $t_0$ is used for all calculations.

For a sufficiently close binary, a self-lensing system would be indistinguishable from a binary undergoing ellipsoidal variations with no self-lensing effect, since the lensing magnification would be so small as to be undetectable.

All simulated light curves are 27 days long (the duration of a TESS sector), and simulated timestamps start at 1 January 2025 and end at 27 January 2025. The absolute timestamp values are of no consequence to the calculation of the simulated photometry, but real timestamps are assigned to simplify the calculation of the time delay in the radial velocity simulations.  The peak time, $t_0$, is randomly selected for each light curve to fall somewhere in the 27 day time window.

Calculation of self-lensing magnifications is performed using the finite-source point-lens formalism of \cite{witt_can_1994}. We use the Einstein radius and impact-parameter convention of \cite{wiktorowicz_predicting_2021}, generalised from the circular-orbit case by replacing the orbital separation with the instantaneous Keplerian lens-source separation,
\begin{equation}
    R_\mathrm{E} = \left(\frac{4 G M_\mathrm{lens} r(t_0) \sin i}{c^2}\right)^{1/2},
    \quad
    b = \frac{a |\cos i|}{R_\mathrm{E}},
    \quad
    b_\mathrm{max} = 1 + \frac{R_\star}{R_\mathrm{E}},
\end{equation}
where $t_0$ is the time of maximum lensing magnification, $r(t_0)$ is the lens-source separation at that time, $a$ is the semi-major axis, and $\omega$ is the phase angle of periapsis relative to $t_0$. For a circular orbit, $r(t_0)=a$ and this reduces to the expression used by \cite{wiktorowicz_predicting_2021}. We define the orbital phase relative to $t_0$ as the true orbital longitude, using the true anomaly $\nu(t)$,
\begin{equation}
    \phi(t) = \omega + \nu(t),
\end{equation}
where $\nu(t)$ is measured relative to periapsis. The true anomaly is obtained from Kepler's equation using the mean anomaly $\mathcal{M}$,
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{M}(t) & = \mathcal{M}_0 + 2\pi\frac{t - t_0}{P_\mathrm{orb}}, \\
    \mathcal{M}(t) & = E(t) - e\sin E(t),                                  \\
    \nu(t)         & = 2\arctan2\left[
        \sqrt{1+e}\sin\frac{E(t)}{2},
        \sqrt{1-e}\cos\frac{E(t)}{2}
        \right],                                                           \\
    P_\mathrm{orb} & =
    2\pi\left(\frac{a^3}{G(M_\mathrm{lens} + M_\star)}\right)^{1/2},
\end{align}
where $e$ is the eccentricity and with $\mathcal{M}_0$ chosen such that $\phi(t_0)=0$. For this orbital phase, the true orbital lens-source separation is
\begin{equation}
    r[\phi(t)] =
    \frac{a(1 - e^2)}
    {1 + e\cos[\phi(t) - \omega]}.
\end{equation}
The projected lens-source separation in Einstein-radius units is
\begin{equation}
    u(t) = \frac{1}{R_\mathrm{E}}
    \left[
        \left(r[\phi(t)] \sin\phi(t)\right)^2
        + \left(b R_\mathrm{E}\right)^2
        \right]^{1/2},
\end{equation}
where $u(t)$ is dimensionless. The self-lensing magnification is calculated with the integrated Witt--Mao finite-source formalism \citep{witt_can_1994}. The implementation evaluates the closed-form uniform-disc Witt--Mao magnification,
\begin{equation}
    A_\mathrm{WM}(u, \rho_\star) =
    \frac{
        C_E E(m) + C_K K(m) + C_\Pi \Pi(n, m)
    }{\pi},
\end{equation}
where $K$, $E$, and $\Pi$ are complete elliptic integrals of the first, second, and third kind. The coefficients $C_E$, $C_K$, and $C_\Pi$, and the elliptic-integral arguments $m$ and $n$, are functions only of the projected lens-source separation and $\rho_\star = R_\star / R_\mathrm{E}$. The disc-integrated magnification used in the simulated light curves is then evaluated by quadrature over a linearly limb-darkened stellar disc,
\begin{align}
    A_\mathrm{SL}(t) & =
    \frac{
        \int_0^1
        A_\mathrm{WM}\left[u(t), \rho\rho_\star\right]
        I(\mu)\,2\rho\,\mathrm{d}\rho
    }{
        \int_0^1 I(\mu)\,2\rho\,\mathrm{d}\rho
    },
    \nonumber                             \\
    \mu              & = \sqrt{1-\rho^2},
    \quad
    I(\mu) = 1 - \gamma(1-\mu),
\end{align}
where $\gamma=0.6$ \citep{claret_limb_2017} is the linear limb-darkening coefficient, which is suitable for Sun-like stars observed by TESS.

Calculation of ellipsoidal variations and Doppler boosting are adapted from \cite{sorabella_modeling_2022}, with the orbital separation and radial velocity generalised to the eccentric case. The ellipsoidal-variation term is
\begin{align}
    A_\mathrm{EV}(t) & =
    -\beta
    \frac{M_\mathrm{lens}}{M_\star}
    \left(\frac{R_\star}{r[\phi(t)]}\right)^3
    \sin^2 i \cos 2\phi(t),
    \nonumber                                                           \\
    \beta            & = 0.15\,\frac{(1 + g)(15 + \gamma)}{3 - \gamma},
\end{align}
where $\beta$ is the ellipsoidal-variation coefficient and $g=0.32$ is the gravity-darkening coefficient for a Sun-like star \citep{lucy_gravity-darkening_1967}.

The Doppler-boosting term is
\begin{equation}
    A_\mathrm{DB}(t) =
    (3 - \alpha)\frac{v_r(t)}{c},
    \quad
    \alpha =
    \frac{e^x(3 - x) - 3}{e^x - 1},
    \quad
    x = \frac{h\nu_\mathrm{TESS}}{k_\mathrm{B}T_\mathrm{eff}},
\end{equation}
where $\nu_\mathrm{TESS}$ is the TESS-band effective frequency and $v_r(t)$ is the source radial velocity. The combined model flux is
\begin{equation}
    F(t) = F_0 A_\mathrm{SL}(t)\left[1 + A_\mathrm{EV}(t) + A_\mathrm{DB}(t)\right].
\end{equation}

\subsection{Radial velocity simulations} \label{sec:rv-sims}

The simulated RV curves start at randomised times between 1 year and 10 years from the end of the photometry. This is to simulate a realistic delay in identifying candidates in the photometry and organising follow-up of those candidates.

The RV simulations use the same physical parameters and phase convention as the photometric simulations. Let $\mu_g=G(M_\mathrm{lens}+M_\star)$ be the standard gravitational parameter, and $h=[\mu_g a(1-e^2)]^{1/2}$ be the specific orbital angular momentum. The source-star barycentric radial and tangential orbital-velocity components are
\begin{align}
    v_{\mathrm{rad},\star}(t) & =
    \frac{M_\mathrm{lens}}{M_\mathrm{lens}+M_\star}
    \frac{\mu_g}{h}
    e\sin\nu(t),
    \nonumber                     \\
    v_{\mathrm{tan},\star}(t) & =
    \frac{M_\mathrm{lens}}{M_\mathrm{lens}+M_\star}
    \frac{\mu_g}{h}
    \left[1 + e\cos\nu(t)\right].
\end{align}
The simulated line-of-sight radial velocity is then
\begin{equation}
    v_r(t) =
    \left[
        v_{\mathrm{tan},\star}(t)\sin\phi(t)
        -
        v_{\mathrm{rad},\star}(t)\cos\phi(t)
        \right]\sin i.
\end{equation}
This reduces to the circular-orbit convention $v_r(t)\propto\sin\phi(t)$ when $e=0$, but for eccentric orbits the radial component means the RV at $t_0$ is not generally zero.

We generate baseline RV curves which contain one RV measurement per day for five years from the start time.

We sample the baseline RV curves to create more realistic RV observations, also adding Gaussian noise to simulate varying instrument accuracies. Each RV curve is resampled using every combination of the parameters in Table~\ref{tab:realistic_observing_grid}, giving $3 \times 3 \times 5 = 45$ realistic observing configurations per baseline simulation. We do not attempt to simulate fully realistic observing (with observing seasons, seeing, varying weather conditions, etc.) as we are mainly interested in measuring the effects of instrument selection, total length of observations, and number of observations.

\begin{table}
    \centering
    \caption{Observing parameters used when creating realistic simulated RV observations.}
    \label{tab:realistic_observing_grid}
    \begin{tabular}{lll}
        \hline
        Parameter   & Description               & Values                    \\
        \hline
        Baseline    & RV length                 & 1, 3, 5 yr                \\
        Cadence     & Time between observations & 1, 30, 300 d              \\
        RV accuracy & Gaussian $\sigma$         & 0.1, 1, 10, 100, 1000 m/s \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

As well as the Gaussian instrumental noise, a fixed stellar-noise term of $\sigma = 10$ m/s is added in every configuration. For simplicity, we do not include proper motion or heliocentric motion, as these would be corrected before fitting real observations.

\subsection{Fitting of simulated observations} \label{sec:fitting}

Each simulated pair of photometric light curves and RV curves is then fitted via MCMC using the BlackJAX package (128 walkers, and 200 burn-in steps). The photometry is clipped to a width of one crossing time around the lensing peak and binned to 100 bins for computational efficiency. Before running the full joint fit (photometry and RV), a photometry-only pre-fit is run for each simulated light curve (1,000 steps) and the walker state from this is used as the initial walker state for the 45 joint fits (an additional 1,000 steps). An RV-only fit of 1,000 steps is also performed. For computational efficiency, these are run as simultaneous vectorised fits on a GPU. We fit only the fundamental physical parameters of the system,
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\theta} = (a, i, M_\mathrm{lens}, e, \omega),
\end{equation}
with the stellar mass, stellar radius, effective temperature, distance, and lensing-peak time held fixed at their simulated values. Orbital period, impact parameter, maximum impact parameter, and effective lensing-event duration are derived from the fitted physical parameters rather than fitted directly. We use a uniform prior within the corresponding parameter limits in Table~\ref{tab:phot_sim_parameter_ranges}, with negative infinity probability outside these limits. We also require trial systems to satisfy the self-lensing geometry condition $b \leq b_\mathrm{max}$.

For a trial parameter vector $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, let $v_\mathrm{mod}(t_k;\boldsymbol{\theta})$ be the model RV at the $k$th RV observation time, and let $N_\mathrm{RV}$ be the number of RV measurements. The RV contribution to the fit statistic is
\begin{equation}
    Q_\mathrm{RV}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) =
    -\frac{1}{N_\mathrm{RV}}
    \sum_{k=1}^{N_\mathrm{RV}}
    \left[
        \frac{v_k - v_\mathrm{mod}(t_k;\boldsymbol{\theta})}
        {\sigma_{\mathrm{RV},k}}
        \right]^2,
\end{equation}
where $v_k$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{RV},k}$ are the simulated RV measurement and uncertainty. For the light curve, let $f_j$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{LC},j}$ be the binned photometric flux and uncertainty at the $j$th binned time, let $f_\mathrm{mod}(t_j;\boldsymbol{\theta})$ be the corresponding model flux, and let $N_\mathrm{LC}$ be the number of fitted light-curve bins. The light-curve contribution is
\begin{equation}
    Q_\mathrm{LC}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) =
    -\frac{1}{N_\mathrm{LC}}
    \sum_{j=1}^{N_\mathrm{LC}}
    \left[
        \frac{f_j - f_\mathrm{mod}(t_j;\boldsymbol{\theta})}
        {\sigma_{\mathrm{LC},j}}
        \right]^2.
\end{equation}
The quantity sampled by the MCMC is then the sum of these terms plus the parameter-prior term, $\ln \pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})$,
\begin{equation}
    \ln p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\mathrm{data}) =
    \ln \pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}) +
    Q_\mathrm{RV}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) +
    Q_\mathrm{LC}(\boldsymbol{\theta}).
\end{equation}

\subsection{Predicted detection rates} \label{sec:pop-synth}
In order to inform expectations about how many self-lensing systems might be correctly characterised as a result of detection in modern photometric surveys, we seek to combine our estimates of the recoverability of the physical properties of self-lensing systems with the detectability estimates of \cite{wiktorowicz_predicting_2021}. One important difference between \cite{wiktorowicz_predicting_2021} and the present work is that they use population synthesis to generate a physically plausible distribution of physical properties, as the basis for simulating the total number of detectable systems observed by each photometric survey. In the present work, we do not simulate a physically representative distribution of parameters, because our primary aim is to explore the ability to recover those parameters regardless of how common a given configuration will be in the galaxy. In other words, we want to simulate very rare configurations as thoroughly as more common ones. We are also concerned only with characterisation of lenses/recovery of physical parameters via spectroscopic RV measurements after detection in photometry, while \cite{wiktorowicz_predicting_2021} investigates photometric detectability.

Still, it is useful to derive from our simulations an estimate of how many systems we should expect to be properly characterised, assuming the predicted number of photometric detections is made in each survey. To this end we build a nearest-neighbour emulator ($k=25$) to map the population synthesis results onto the fitting/recovery results for our radial velocity simulations. For each population-synthesis system and each RV simulation we define a scaled feature vector
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x} =
    \left(
    \frac{\log_{10} M_\mathrm{lens} - \mu_M}{s_M},
    \frac{\log_{10} P_\mathrm{orb} - \mu_P}{s_P},
    \frac{e - \mu_e}{s_e},
    \frac{b - \mu_b}{s_b}
    \right),
\end{equation}
where the centring constants $\mu$ are medians and the scale factors $s$ are the 16th--84th percentile ranges, giving a robust measure of the central spread of each coordinate. Coordinates are centred and scaled to normalise the ranges of the parameters to be similar in parameter space, to prevent the nearest-neighbour search from being dominated by those with the largest numerical spread. For a population-synthesis system $j$, the emulator identifies the set $\mathcal{N}_k(j)$ of the $k$ nearest RV simulations in this scaled space and assigns inverse-distance weights
\begin{equation}
    w_{ij} =
    \frac{(d_{ij} + \epsilon)^{-1}}
    {\sum_{\ell \in \mathcal{N}_k(j)} (d_{\ell j} + \epsilon)^{-1}},
    \quad i \in \mathcal{N}_k(j),
\end{equation}
where $d_{ij} = \lVert\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j\rVert$ and $\epsilon = 10^{-12}$ is a small numerical constant to avoid division by zero. For each observing strategy and recovery metric, the emulated recovery probability is then
\begin{equation}
    \hat{p}_{j} =
    \frac{\sum_{i \in \mathcal{N}_k(j)} v_i w_{ij} q_i}
    {\sum_{i \in \mathcal{N}_k(j)} v_i w_{ij}},
    \qquad
    C_j = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{N}_k(j)} v_i w_{ij},
\end{equation}
where $q_i$ is the measured RV outcome and $v_i$ indicates whether that neighbouring simulation has a valid fit for the relevant strategy. The associated coverage $C_j$ is therefore the fraction of the neighbour weight supported by valid fitted simulations. We emulate three outcomes in this way: correct compact-object class, lens-mass recovery to 10 per cent, and orbital-period recovery to 10 per cent. The final population-weighted fractions are obtained by averaging these emulated probabilities over the detected population-synthesis systems using their population weights $W_j$,
\begin{equation}
    \bar{p} = \frac{\sum_j W_j \hat{p}_j}{\sum_j W_j}.
\end{equation}

We adopt the following mass bins and object classes: $<3 \text{ M}_\odot$ (white dwarfs and neutron stars, grouped together for this analysis); $3-5 \text{ M}_\odot$ (mass gap); $5-20 \text{ M}_\odot$ (lower mass black holes); $20-50 \text{ M}_\odot$ (higher mass black holes; separated from the lower mass to assess how black hole mass affects recovery). We caveat this analysis by noting that our simulations include relatively fewer low mass lenses than high mass lenses, however these results are still informative about our ability to recover masses in these ranges.

\section{Results and discussion}
We evaluate the fits based on their ability to recover the true simulated parameters, with particular interest in lens mass and orbital period. The following subsections evaluate the fitting results in terms of recovering the accurate lens mass, recovering the correct compact object class regardless of the accuracy of the fitted mass (i.e. can we distinguish black holes from white dwarfs and neutron stars, as this distinction affects planning for further follow-up observations; we adopt four object categories: white dwarfs/neutron stars, BH mass gap, lower mass BH, and higher mass BH), recovering the accurate orbital period (using the same 10\% threshold as the lens mass), and then we evaluate the efficacy of fitting photometry alone versus jointly fitting photometry and RV, assessing how few RV measurements are required to achieve the desired recovery accuracy. Finally we use these results to emulate recovery rates for the synthesised population from \cite{wiktorowicz_predicting_2021}, to estimate the number of detected systems which can be correctly characterised for each survey.

It is worth noting that these results represent an ideal best case scenario for obtaining follow-up observations. We aim to assess a representative range of observing schedules, rather than rigorously simulating realistic observations. We do not account for observing seasons, we assume every target is always observable, we do not simulate weather or bad seeing, and we assume only a simple model of noise in the RV measurements (varying amounts of Gaussian noise to simulate spectrograph accuracy, ranging from sub-metre per second accuracy up to kilometre per second accuracy, to represent the range of spectrograph facilities currently available), plus a fixed 10 m/s Gaussian stellar noise). We also assume the exact time of the lensing peak ($t_0$) is known precisely, along with the properties of the stellar companion (mass, radius, effective temperature, and distance from Earth); in practice these would all be subject to some amount of uncertainty.

\subsection{Recovering object class}
While the ultimate goal of our observing campaign will be to accurately measure the lens mass and other physical properties of the population of self-lensing systems, we anticipate having to grapple with many orders of magnitude more false positives than true self-lensing systems. This situation will therefore require an informed, systematic way of triaging and prioritising targets for follow-up. The volume of false positives is anticipated to be so large that we will want to obtain as few observations of each target as possible in order to characterise what type of compact object the lens is (or, indeed, whether each target is even a self-lensing system to begin with). in the present work we assume every target is \textit{some} kind of self-lensing system, but even with that assumption, some classes of object will be more astrophysically interesting than others, and some will require more or fewer observations in order to confirm.

It is therefore informative to know whether we can accurately predict the \textit{class} of the lens, even in cases where we can not accurately predict the mass. Being able to do so will allow us to allocate resources accordingly when we plan follow-up observations.

\subsection{Recovering orbital period}

The orbital period strongly influences the shape of the RV curve. Not only that but it also determines the absolute RV at a given time, because the photometric event tells us the orbital phase at a given time. In the circular-orbit limit the RV is zero at the lensing event and reaches an extremum half an orbit later; for the eccentric systems fitted here, eccentricity and periapsis phase shift the detailed RV shape and extrema. Measuring the change in RV, even over a partial orbit, therefore can tightly constrain the orbital period even for very long orbits.

Figure~\ref{fig:orbital-period-recovery-by-period} shows the percentage of systems whose physical parameters could be recovered, as a function of cadence, baseline, RV accuracy, and orbital period. There is a clear dependence upon observing cadence and observing baseline for the recovery of the parameters. In all cases, recovery is worse for the shortest orbital period range than for the 2.5--18 year range. This suggests the observing cadence needs to be frequent enough to repeatedly sample a single orbit, rather than sampling multiple orbits. For the shortest orbits, a longer observing baseline actually makes recovery worse; this suggests the model is confused when presented with many sparse measurements of multiple orbits (\textbf{TODO: See if this is still true once the final figures are in}).

RV accuracy also has a measurable effect, but this varies by orbital period. This suggests that in many cases, even kilometre scale accuracy is sufficient to recover the parameters, and improving this further does not improve recovery. However, for longer orbital periods the recovery becomes more sensitive to RV accuracy. This is due to the smaller rate of change of the RV in these systems; a. more accurate spectrograph is required to detect the relatively small change in RV over the course of a few years when the orbital period is decades or centuries long. While challenging, however, this does not completely prevent such systems from being characterised. With sufficiently accurate RV measurements we can characterise self-lensing systems with orbital periods of many centuries.

\subsection{Photometry-only and RV-only vs joint fits and $N_\text{RV}$}

These simulations are generated on the assumption that we photometrically detect a candidate self-lensing microlensing event and then follow up with RV measurements some time later. In order to optimise the RV observing schedule we therefore explore the question of how informative the photometry is alone and whether information derived from the photometry can be used in planning RV observations. For completeness, we also assess how well we can recover parameters with RV only.

\begin{figure*}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{paper/figures/prefit_improvement_by_n_rv.pdf}
    \caption{Comparison between the photometry-only pre-fit accuracy, RV-only fit accuracy, and joint photometry plus RV fit accuracy for all fitted rows with saved pre-fit and RV-only values. Separate panels show the fitted parameters. Points show the median relative error in each parameter as a function of the number of RV measurements, and shaded regions show the interquartile range. The horizontal dotted line marks the 0.1 relative-error recovery threshold.}
    \label{fig:prefit-improvement-by-n-rv}
\end{figure*}

Figure~\ref{fig:prefit-improvement-by-n-rv} shows the median improvement of fitted parameters (in terms of the relative error in the fitted values) as a function of the number of RV measurements. In every case, carrying out a joint photometric-RV fit results in the most accurate recovery of the simulation parameters, and RV alone is better than photometry alone. For all parameters, the median relative error is less than unity even with a photometry-only fit. The addition of just two RV measurements substantially improves recovery for lens mass, semi-major axis, and orbital period, and the relative error levels off after five RV measurements (there is some scatter in the values/it appears non-monotonic; \textbf{TODO: Explain this}). The improvement in the impact parameter is more modest, but the addition of RV does still result in an improvement in accuracy, especially for the examples which fit the worst with photometry alone (as shown by the reduction in the upper quartile).

\begin{figure*}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{paper/figures/prefit_improvement_by_lens_mass_range.pdf}
    \caption{Comparison between photometry-only pre-fit accuracy, RV-only fit accuracy, and joint photometry plus RV fit accuracy, separated by true lens-mass range. Rows show fitted parameters and columns show true lens-mass ranges. Points show the median relative error as a function of the number of RV measurements, shaded regions show the interquartile range, and the horizontal dotted line marks the 0.1 relative-error recovery threshold.}
    \label{fig:prefit-improvement-by-lens-mass-range}
\end{figure*}

Figure~\ref{fig:prefit-improvement-by-lens-mass-range} shows the median improvement of fitted parameters broken down by the compact object class. It is informative to know whether we can correctly determine which type of compact object is the lens, even if the mass is not well constrained, so that we can allocate future observing resources accordingly. Impact parameter is better constrained for lower mass lenses (i.e. smaller inter-quartile range), which is due to these systems having smaller Einstein ring radii (a smaller Einstein ring radius requires an inclination closer to edge-on to produce detectable lensing). For the other parameters the improvement with additional RV measurements is slightly better for higher mass systems. The most interesting feature of this figure is the effect of true lens mass on the accuracy of the photometry-only fitted mass. The fitted mass is more accurate for higher mass lenses, and for the highest mass bin is almost as accurate as with the addition of RV. In other words, higher mass black holes are easier to confirm with only photometry and RV is more decisive when confirming lower mass black holes, neutron stars, and white dwarfs.

\begin{figure*}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{paper/figures/prefit_compact_object_class_by_lens_mass_range.pdf}
    \caption{Compact-object class recovery for the photometry-only pre-fit, RV-only fit, and joint photometry plus RV fit, separated by true lens-mass range. A system is counted as correct when the fitted lens mass falls in the same broad compact-object class as the true mass, using $M_\mathrm{acc}<3\,M_\odot$ for white dwarf or neutron-star lenses, $3 \leq M_\mathrm{acc}/M_\odot < 5$ for the mass gap, and $M_\mathrm{acc}\geq5\,M_\odot$ for black-hole lenses. Points show the percentage of fitted systems in the correct class as a function of the number of RV measurements; the horizontal dotted line marks 80 per cent.}
    \label{fig:prefit-compact-object-class-by-lens-mass-range}
\end{figure*}

Figure~\ref{fig:prefit-compact-object-class-by-lens-mass-range} shows the percentage of systems which fall into the correct compact object mass bin regardless of the accuracy of the fitted mass. There is a clear trend where higher mass objects can be more accurately determined to be such, even if their exact mass can not be constrained. A higher mass black hole creates such a prominent lensing curve that it can be identified more easily, whereas neutron stars and white dwarfs are more ambiguous. IT may be surprising that the photometry-only fits look so much worse foe low mass lenses in Figure~\ref{fig:prefit-compact-object-class-by-lens-mass-range} than in Figure~\ref{fig:prefit-improvement-by-lens-mass-range}; remember that Figure~\ref{fig:prefit-improvement-by-lens-mass-range} shows the relative error, with a median error close to 50\% for the smallest mass bin. An error of that scale is more likely to result in lower mass systems falling in the wrong mass bin than larger mass systems (which have wider bins in this analysis). This is important astrophysically, as it makes it harder to distinguish neutron stars from mass gap black holes from photometry alone; for such candidates it is therefore more important to obtain RV measurements in order to characterise the nature of the lens.

Figure~\ref{fig:population-weighted-rv-followup-heatmaps-counts-macc} shows the recovery rates for lens mass. Lens-mass recovery to within 10\% is much harder than class recovery (18.8--53.2\%, with a mean of 39.2\%). We can determine the class more easily than the accurate mass, so a sensible strategy would be to obtain a small number of RV observations to determine the likely object class, and then prioritise further RV observations based on those results to accuratrly determine the mass for the most interesting systems. Observing baseline has a large effect on the recovery rate, with the mean rising from 27.8 per cent at 1 yr to 42.4 per cent at 3 yr and 47.5 per cent at 5 yr. The 300 d cadence rows are generally worse than 1 or 30 d cadence, so observing cadence will also be an important consideration.
