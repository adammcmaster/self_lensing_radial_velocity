\section{Introduction}

Self-lensing systems binary systems which are close to being edge-on, consisting of a star in orbit with a compact object. With an appropriate separation distance, the Einstein radius of the compact object can be larger than its surface radius, and if the orbital inclination is close enough to $\frac{1}{2}\pi$ this can result in a measurable microlensing amplification of the stellar flux once per orbit.

To date, five self-lensing binaries have been confirmed, each containing a white dwarf-stellar binary, all of which have relatively close, short-period orbits. However, it is expected that stellar-compact object binaries should span the full range of masses of such objects, as well as the full range of orbital configurations.

photometry remains the best way to detect self-lensing binaries, and previous works have assessed the detectability of self-lensing binaries in various photometric surveys. These results look promising, with modern all-sky surveys expected to imminently uncover a population of compact objects in self-lensing binaries, including the previously unstudied population of quiescent black holes.

While detection of self-lensing systems with photometry alone has been demonstrated, modelling of such systems and characterisation of the compact component is subject to numerous degeneracies between the physical parameters which prevent the mass from being determined unless the star's radial velocity can be measured. For short-period binaries it should be possible to derive a photometric RV by modelling Doppler boosting (CITATIONS, including COBALT-BLUE), but for systems with no detectable Doppler boosting effect, spectroscopic observations are required.

In this work, we therefore consider the question of how many spectroscopic RV measurements are required, and at what level of precision, in order to correctly model the self-lensing system parameters, as a function of those parameters, including systems with orbital periods on the order of centuries. This will inform observing strategies for follow-up of the large number of candidates expected to come from new surveys, allowing the most efficient use of resources by not obtaining more observations than necessary.

We take a library of simulated TESS photometry of self-lensing systems (which was produced as part of work to be published in a separate paper, currently in prep), and generate simulated radial velocity curves for the same input parameters. We simulate a range of observing strategies, varying observing campaign duration (baseline), frequency of observations (cadence), and accuracy of RV measruements. We then fit a self-lensing model jointly to the photometry and radial velocity curves to attempt to recover the physical properties of each self-lensing system (the lens mass, semi-major axis, inclination angle, eccentricity, and periapsis phase angle). We assume the stellar properties (mass, radius effective temperature, and distance from Earth) are known.

We will show that it is possible to model very long period self-lensing binaries with a handful of RV measurements. Photometric-only fits can come close to determining the properties, and the photometry gives us the orbital phase and narrows down the inclination, so that we don't need many RV measurements to fit everything.

The main distinguishing feature of self-lensing vs microlensing is the periodicity of the lensing flares. To claim photometric discovery of a self-lensing system, ideally we would aim to detect this periodicity.

It's worth really stressing how unique self-lensing binaries are when it comes to determining the lens mass. Spectroscopically, they're like SB1s but with the possibility of jointly fitting photometry. They're to SB1s as eclipsing binaries are to SB2s. The photometry can possibly give us the orbital period, and a single event gives us information about the orbital configuration and lens mass via the amplitude, duration, and shape of the lensing curve. By including limb darkening in the model, we potentially learn even more about the semi-major axis, as limb darkening effects will be more pronounced for closer orbits.

The aim of this work is to quantitfy the observational requirements for radial velocity (RV) meadurements that will allow us to determine the lens mass and orbital configuration of self-lensing binary candidates discovered via our ongoing photometric search. WE investigate the relationship between observing cadence, observing campaign duration (baseline), and accuracy of RV measurements, and the rate of recovery of phyiscal parameters as a fuction of lens mass. We investigate recovery rate by both accurate lens mass (determined to within 10\% of the true value) and broad compact object class (i.e. distinguishing black holes from other compact objects even if the recovered mass deviates from the true mass by more than 10 \%).

We use as the basis of this work a library of self-lensing photometric simulations produced in another work. These simulations are described in brief in Section~\ref{sec:phot-sims}. A full description of how these were generated will be published in a future work (currently in prep). The photometric simulations are loaded and then radial velocity measurements are simulated for the same set of simulation parameters (Section~\ref{sec:rv-sims}). The RV simulations are then sampled according to a range of observign schedules, and Gaussian noise is added to simnulate a range of spectrograph accuracies (Section~\ref{sec:rv-realistic}). An MCMC fit is then performed to find the self-lensing physical parameters which jointly best fit the photometry and RV (Section~\ref{sec:fitting}). We then aggregate the recovery rates across observing parameters and self-lensing physical parameters to investigate the effect of each parameter on the recovery rate.

\subsection{Photometric simulations} \label{sec:phot-sims}

These simulations comprise a sample of self-lensing system parameters which is physically complete (i.e. covering the full plausible range of physical parameters) but not realistic (i.e. parameters are not sampled according to the frequency they are expected to occur in nature). This is ideal as the basis for determining the ranges of parameters which produce self-lensing effects which are detectable, independent of how common or uncommon particular parameter ranges are expected to be.

The library of simulations includes realistic simulated TESS light curves (including stellar noise generated with a Gaussian process) for each sample self-lensing system, stored alongside the original parameter values. When generating the simulations, the parameter values were sampled uniformly between the following limits, with the exception of semi-major axis and eccentricity which are sampled on a log scale to favour smaller values:

        \hline
        $a$               & Semi-major axis       & 0.1--200 AU               \\
        $i$               & Orbital inclination   & $0.4999\pi$--$0.5\pi$ rad \\
        $M_\mathrm{lens}$ & Lens mass             & 0.5--50 M$_\odot$         \\
        $M_\star$         & Stellar mass          & 0.5--50 M$_\odot$         \\
        $e$               & Eccentricity          & 0--0.999999               \\
        $\omega$          & Periapsis phase angle & 0--$2\pi$                 \\
        $d$               & Distance from Earth   & 0.1--100 kpc              \\

    \end{tabular}

The full library of simulations actually includes both lensing and non-lensing light curves. For this work, we select only the ``lensing'' subset from the library of simulations; i.e. we use only the simulated light curves which contain visible lensing peaks, where the peak amplitude is larger than the photometric noise. This subset excludes systems overflowing their Roche lobe and any systems where lensing is not geometrically possible.

Three photometric effects are included when generating the light curves: self-lensing, ellipsoidal variations, and Doppler boosting. At small values of $a$ the light curve is dominated by ellipsoidal variations and Doppler boosting (though lensing is often visible too); at large $a$ lensing is dominant, with ellipsoidal variation and Doppler boosting tending to zero as $a$ increases.

Calculation of self-lensing magnifications is performed using the finite-source point-lens formalism of \cite{witt_can_1994}. We use the Einstein radius and impact-parameter convention of \cite{wiktorowicz_predicting_2021}, generalised from the circular-orbit case by replacing the orbital separation with the instantaneous Keplerian lens-source separation,
\begin{equation}
    R_\mathrm{E} = \left(\frac{4 G M_\mathrm{lens} r(t_0) \sin i}{c^2}\right)^{1/2},
    \quad
    b = \frac{a |\cos i|}{R_\mathrm{E}},
    \quad
    b_\mathrm{max} = 1 + \frac{R_\star}{R_\mathrm{E}},
\end{equation}
where $t_0$ is the time of maximum lensing magnification and $r(t_0)$ is the lens-source separation at that time. For a circular orbit, $r(t_0)=a$ and this reduces to the expression used by \cite{wiktorowicz_predicting_2021}. We define the orbital phase relative to $t_0$ as the true orbital longitude, using the true anomaly $\nu(t)$,
\begin{equation}
    \phi(t) = \omega + \nu(t),
\end{equation}
where $\nu(t)$ is measured relative to periapsis. The true anomaly is obtained from Kepler's equation using the mean anomaly $\mathcal{M}$,
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{M}(t) & = \mathcal{M}_0 + 2\pi\frac{t - t_0}{P_\mathrm{orb}}, \\
    \mathcal{M}(t) & = E(t) - e\sin E(t),                                  \\
    \nu(t)         & = 2\arctan2\left[
        \sqrt{1+e}\sin\frac{E(t)}{2},
        \sqrt{1-e}\cos\frac{E(t)}{2}
    \right],                                                               \\
    P_\mathrm{orb} & =
    2\pi\left(\frac{a^3}{G(M_\mathrm{lens} + M_\star)}\right)^{1/2},
\end{align}
with $\mathcal{M}_0$ chosen such that $\phi(t_0)=0$. For this orbital phase, the true orbital lens-source separation is
\begin{equation}
    r[\phi(t)] =
    \frac{a(1 - e^2)}
    {1 + e\cos[\phi(t) - \omega]}.
\end{equation}
Here $a$ is the semi-major axis, $e$ is the eccentricity, and $\omega$ is the phase angle of periapsis relative to $t_0$. The projected lens-source separation in Einstein-radius units is
\begin{equation}
    u(t) = \frac{1}{R_\mathrm{E}}
    \left[
        \left(r[\phi(t)] \sin\phi(t)\right)^2
        + \left(b R_\mathrm{E}\right)^2
        \right]^{1/2},
\end{equation}
where $u(t)$ is dimensionless. The self-lensing magnification is calculated with the integrated Witt--Mao finite-source formalism \citep{witt_can_1994}. The implementation evaluates the closed-form uniform-disc Witt--Mao magnification,
\begin{equation}
    A_\mathrm{WM}(u, \rho_\star) =
    \frac{
        C_E E(m) + C_K K(m) + C_\Pi \Pi(n, m)
    }{\pi},
\end{equation}
where $K$, $E$, and $\Pi$ are complete elliptic integrals of the first, second, and third kind. The coefficients $C_E$, $C_K$, and $C_\Pi$, and the elliptic-integral arguments $m$ and $n$, are functions only of the projected lens-source separation and $\rho_\star = R_\star / R_\mathrm{E}$. The disc-integrated magnification used in the simulated light curves is then evaluated by quadrature over a linearly limb-darkened stellar disc,
\begin{align}
    A_\mathrm{SL}(t) & =
    \frac{
        \int_0^1
        A_\mathrm{WM}\left[u(t), \rho\rho_\star\right]
        I(\mu)\,2\rho\,\mathrm{d}\rho
    }{
        \int_0^1 I(\mu)\,2\rho\,\mathrm{d}\rho
    },
    \nonumber                             \\
    \mu              & = \sqrt{1-\rho^2},
    \quad
    I(\mu) = 1 - \gamma(1-\mu),
\end{align}
with the same limb-darkening coefficient $\gamma=0.6$ used below for the ellipsoidal-variation term.

Calculation of ellipsoidal variations and Doppler boosting are adapted from \cite{sorabella_modeling_2022}, with the orbital separation generalised to the eccentric case. The ellipsoidal-variation term is
\begin{align}
    A_\mathrm{EV}(t) & =
    -\beta
    \frac{M_\mathrm{lens}}{M_\star}
    \left(\frac{R_\star}{r[\phi(t)]}\right)^3
    \sin^2 i \cos 2\phi(t),
    \nonumber                                                           \\
    \beta            & = 0.15\,\frac{(1 + g)(15 + \gamma)}{3 - \gamma},
\end{align}
where $\beta$ is the ellipsoidal-variation coefficient, calculated using gravity-darkening coefficient $g=0.4$ and limb-darkening coefficient $\gamma=0.6$. The Doppler-boosting term is
\begin{equation}
    A_\mathrm{DB}(t) =
    (3 - \alpha)\frac{v_r(t)}{c},
    \quad
    \alpha =
    \frac{\exp(x)(3 - x) - 3}{\exp(x) - 1},
    \quad
    x = \frac{h\nu_\mathrm{TESS}}{k_\mathrm{B}T_\mathrm{eff}},
\end{equation}
where $\nu_\mathrm{TESS}$ is the TESS-band effective frequency and $v_r(t)$ is the source radial velocity. The combined model flux is
\begin{equation}
    F(t) = F_0 A_\mathrm{SL}(t)\left[1 + A_\mathrm{EV}(t) + A_\mathrm{DB}(t)\right].
\end{equation}
These effects all depend on the orbital phase at a given time during the simulation. The zero point for this is defined as the moment of maximum lensing magnification (i.e. the time of minimum projected separation between source and lens, $t_0$). The periapsis phase angle is defined in terms of this zero point; i.e. the phase angle at which periastron passage occurs, relative to the position at $t_0$. The source-lens distance is calculated for each time according to the phase angle at that time, meaning the source-lens distance changes during the microlensing event according to the eccentricity. Strictly speaking, the Einstein ring radius should also change as a function of orbital phase, but this is not accounted for in the present work. Instead, the radius at $t_0$ is used for all calculations.

For a sufficiently close binary, a self-lensing system would be indistinguishable from a binary undergoing ellipsoidal variations with no self-lensing effect, since the lensing magnification would so small as to be undetectable. Indeed the only physical difference is the angle of inclination; there is no inherent difference between the two classes of system. In this work we consider only systems which are are close enough to edge-on to create a lensing effect.

All simulated light curves start at 1 January 2025 and end at 27 January 2025. The peak time, i.e. $\phi = 0$, is randomly selected for each light curve to fall somewhere between this time window.

\subsection{Radial velocity simulations} \label{sec:rv-sims}

The simulated RV curves start at randomised times between 1 year and 10 years from the end of the photometry. This is to simulate a realistic delay in identifying candidates in the photometry and organising follow-up of those candidates.

The RV simulations use the same physical parameters and phase convention as the photometric simulations. Let $\nu(t)=\phi(t)-\omega$ be the true anomaly relative to periapsis, $\mu=G(M_\mathrm{lens}+M_\star)$, and $h=[\mu a(1-e^2)]^{1/2}$. The source-star barycentric radial and tangential orbital-velocity components are
\begin{align}
    v_{\mathrm{rad},\star}(t) & =
    \frac{M_\mathrm{lens}}{M_\mathrm{lens}+M_\star}
    \frac{\mu}{h}
    e\sin\nu(t),
    \nonumber                     \\
    v_{\mathrm{tan},\star}(t) & =
    \frac{M_\mathrm{lens}}{M_\mathrm{lens}+M_\star}
    \frac{\mu}{h}
    \left[1 + e\cos\nu(t)\right].
\end{align}
The simulated line-of-sight radial velocity is then
\begin{equation}
    v_r(t) =
    \left[
        v_{\mathrm{tan},\star}(t)\sin\phi(t)
        -
        v_{\mathrm{rad},\star}(t)\cos\phi(t)
        \right]\sin i.
\end{equation}
This reduces to the circular-orbit convention $v_r(t)\propto\sin\phi(t)$ when $e=0$, but for eccentric orbits the radial component means the RV at $t_0$ is not generally zero.

We generate baseline RV curves which contain one RV measurement per day for five years from the start time.

We evaluate the fits based on their ability to recover the true simulated parameters, with partocular interest in lens mass and orbital period. Note that orbital period is not one of the model parameters (our Keplerian self-lensing model includes only the fudamental phsyical properties of the binary), but is derived here from the fitted lens mass and semi-major axis. Fitting the physical properies makes the most sense when modelling the lensing effect, while orbital period is significant when planning RV observations.

The following subsections evaluate the fitting results in terms of recovering the accurate lens mass (we adopt a threshold of 10\% to denote ``accurate'' recovery), recovering the correct compact object class regardless of the accuracy of the fitted mass (i.e. can we distinguish black holes from white dwarfs and neutron stars, as this distinction affects plannign for further followup observations; we adopt four object categories: white dwarfs/neutron stars, BH mass gap, lower mass BH, and higher mass BH), recovering the accurate orbital period (using the same 10\% threshold as the lens mass), and then finally we evaluate the efficacy of fitting photometry alone versus jointly fitting photometry and RV, assessing how few RV measurements are required to achieve the desired recovery accuracy.

It is owrth noting that these results represent an ideal best case scenario for obtaining followup observations. We aim to assess a representative range of observing schedules, rather than rigorously simulating realistic observations. We do not account for observing seasons, we assume every target is always observable, we do not simnulate weather or bad seeing, and we assume only a simple model of noise in the RV measurements (varying amounts of Gaussian noise to simulate spectrograph accuracy, ranging from sub-metre per second accuracy up to kilometre per second accuracy, to represent the range of spectrograph facilities currenty available), plus a fixed 10 m/s Gaussian stellar noise). We also assume the exact time of the lensing peak ($t_0$) is known precisely, along iwth the properties of the stellar companion (mass, radius, effective temperature, and distance from Earth); in practice these would all be subject to some amount of uncertainty.

While the ultimate goal of our observing campaign will be to accurately measure tge lens mass and other physical properties of th epopulation of self-lensing systems, we anticipate having to grapple with many orders of magnitude more false positives than true self-lensing systems. This situation will therefore require an informed, systematic way of triaging and prioritising taregts for followup. The volume of false positives is anticipated to be so large that we will want to obtain as few observations of each target as possible in order to characterise what type of compact object the lens is (or, indeed, whether each tatget is even a self-lensing system to begin with). in the present work we assume every target is \textit{some} kind of selflensing system, but even with that assumption, some classes of object will be more astrophysically interesting than others, and some will require more or fewer observations in order to confirm.

\subsection{Recovering orbital period}

The orbital period strongly influences the shape of the RV curve. Not only that but it also determines the absolute RV at a given time post-lensing, because the photometric event tells us the orbital phase at a given time. In the circular-orbit limit the RV is zero at the lensing event and reaches an extremum half an orbit later; for the eccentric systems fitted here, eccentricity and periapsis phase shift the detailed RV shape and extrema. Measuring the change in RV, even over a parital orbit, therefore can tightly constrain the orbital period even for very long orbits.
